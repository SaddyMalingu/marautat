The conversion engine

Status: shipped 2026-10-29. Files: utils/conversionPlaybook.js (new), utils/ctaBlock.js (new), utils/blogWriter.js, utils/blogQuality.js, utils/contentJourney.js, scripts/blogManager.js, scripts/smartAutomation.js.

The problem this fixes

The blog was well written and did not convert. Two measurements say why.

1. The Mercor referral funnel (rolling window)

| Step | Count | Step conversion | |---|---|---| | Signed up | 26 | — | | Applications started | 159 | — | | Applications completed | 22 | 13.8% of started | | Offers extended | 4 | 18.2% | | Hired | 3 | 75% | | Paid | 0 | — |

Six of every seven people who wanted the work gave up inside the application. Nothing on the page had told them what the form would ask for, so the form was the first time they met the requirements. Total referrals paid to date: $566.32.

2. The LinkedIn export (48 articles, 2025-10-27 to 2026-10-26)

| Pattern | Posts | Impressions | Median | |---|---|---|---| | Situational story ("the shift towards a digital world") | 1 | 88,314 | 88,314 | | "Unlock your future as a …" | 1 | 25 | 25 | | "Master the … assessment" | 1 | 13 | 13 | | "Ready to work as a …" | 1 | 2 | 2 | | All 48 posts | 48 | 100,397 | 91 |

One post took 88% of all impressions and 92% of all link clicks (679 of 741). Every title built as a command or a promise reached between 0 and 25 people.

What changed

utils/conversionPlaybook.js — one playbook per offer type

Six types (job, product, service, brand, affiliate, course). Each has:

  • reader — who is actually reading
  • decision — the single action the page sells
  • blocker — why they hesitate (for jobs: *"they do not know what the

    • application will demand, so the form is where they find out and quit"*)

  • proof — the three things that move them
  • readiness — what they must have ready before they click
  • afterClick — what happens, in order
  • askEarly / askMid / askClose — the ask ladder

Also exports WEAK_TITLE_PATTERNS (the losing title shapes, with the LinkedIn evidence in the reason string), TITLE_SHAPES (what to write instead), conversionBrief() (prompt text) and readinessCard() (deterministic HTML).

utils/blogWriter.js — the brief now carries the conversion job

buildArticlePrompt() gains a THE CONVERSION JOB section and shapeRules() gains rules 9-12:

  • 9. first 100 words: offer, figure, next step
  • 10. mid-page ask around section four
  • 11. answer the named hesitation in the body
  • 12. never invent the middle of the journey (no invented assessment format,

    • delivery promise, guarantee or processing time)

buildRepairPrompt() chases the same targets, so the repair pass cannot loop on a page that keeps failing for a reason the repair prompt never mentioned.

utils/blogQuality.js — the gate measures conversion

analyzeArticle(html, { offerType, title }) now also returns conversion:

| Field | Meaning | |---|---| | offerInOpening | a figure, price or next step in the first 110 words | | midAsk | a call to action between 30% and 75% of the page | | readinessHeading | a "Before you apply/order/enrol/get in touch" section, matching the type's verb | | firstPerson | how often the business says we/our/us | | distinctNumbers | unique figures on the page | | duplicateParagraphs | paragraphs repeated word for word | | weakTitle | the title is a promise, not a situation |

New issues: duplicate-paragraph (weight 12, blocking), no-readiness (10), no-offer-early (8), no-mid-ask (6), thin-specifics (6), no-first-person (4), weak-title (0, warning). The readiness check is type-aware: a job page saying "Before you get in touch" is a buyer template with the nouns swapped, and it fails.

utils/ctaBlock.js — the button says something

Every page used to end with Ready to Start? / This opportunity is available now. / [Apply Now] — on a job, a product and a course alike — linking to /ai-jobs/apply/<id> even when the offer had no destination, which returns {"error":"No destination configured for this offer"}.

Now: the heading names the offer ("Apply for ML Engineer", "Get Studio Session priced"), the figure and place are printed when the offer has them, the readiness list sits directly above the button, and there is a compact copy above the article so a reader who never scrolls still has a way out. Destination order is destination_url → referral_url → application_url → /ai-jobs/apply/<id>. With none of those, no button is rendered — the page asks for a message instead of sending the reader to a 404.

OFFER_CTA no longer maps service → "Learn More".

utils/contentJourney.js — titles that earn the click

Job titles stopped being commands and stopped carrying a trailing year or a possessive s (which produced "…English & Finnishes" on live pages):

| Before | After | |---|---| | What is a X? A Complete Guide for 2026 | What a X Actually Does All Day | | X Salary Guide: What You Can Actually Earn | Is $75/hr Really What a X Earns? | | Essential Skills for Xs: Do You Have What It Takes? | The Skills X Work Actually Rewards | | How to Pass the X Assessment: Complete Prep Guide | What the X Assessment Asks For, and How to Prepare | | How to Get Hired as a X: Step-by-Step | Applying for X Work: What Happens, Step by Step | | Remote X Jobs: Work from Anywhere Guide | Where Remote X Jobs Are Actually Listed | | X Career Roadmap: Junior to Senior in 5 Years | What Changes in Five Years of X Work |

scripts/blogManager.js — publishing hygiene

  • verify-writers-flow-* and test-ai-specialist-* rows are written as

    • drafts and get no static page. 36 of them were live in the public index.

  • The pipeline passes { offerType, title } into analyzeArticle(), so the

    • conversion gate actually runs.

The boot migration (2026-10-29, after the first deploy)

restoreFromDB() copied html_content byte-for-byte whenever a page passed the readability audit, so the new footer reached only newly written pages. Three changes make the existing corpus migrate itself on boot, with no model calls:

  1. A page still carrying Ready to Start? or This opportunity is available now.

    2. is rebuilt even though it passes the audit — no code path produces those strings any more, so the page is stale by definition (isStalePage() in utils/ctaBlock.js).

  2. The offers query the rebuild uses now selects compensation_max,

    3. compensation_currency, location_text, destination_url, referral_url and application_url, so the new footer can print the figure, the place and the real destination.

  3. /blog is built from the files on disk, so any file without a published row

    4. kept being advertised — and the verifier's own pages were 93 links deep in the index, titled Verify Writers Flow 20261022130935. The restore now demotes existing verifier rows to draft, deletes files with no published post, and rebuilds the index from real titles instead of slug-cased filenames.

First deploy result: Restored 337 posts (240 rebuilt from their article body). Six sampled pages verified live — no legacy block, offer named in the heading, readiness list present, two buttons.

The second gap, and the fixed point (2026-10-29, evening)

That migration only knew the old footer, so the pages that had never had one were invisible to it: 96 of the 308 live pages carried no closing block at all, and 21 carried one whose only content was a sentence. Neither state fails the readability audit, so restoreFromDB() copied them byte-for-byte on every boot.

pageNeedsRebuild() in utils/ctaBlock.js is now the whole decision — audit failure, old footer, missing block, nothing to press — and the boot restore, the repair script and the tests all call it, so no copy of the rule can drift:


pageNeedsRebuild({ html, auditOk, issues, mayRebuildFooter })
// → { rebuild: true, reason: 'no closing block' }

The reason is what the boot log prints ([DB] Rebuilt <slug> (no closing block)), so an operator sees which rule fired without cross-referencing the audit.

The rule this path lives or dies by: a predicate that disagrees with the template rewrites the same pages on every boot, and nobody notices because the site still looks fine. ctaBlock()'s no-destination branch used to emit a block with nothing to press — exactly what the predicate calls broken — so it now ends with a link to /ai-jobs. npm run test:boot-convergence proves the fixed point offline: it boots a corpus of stored pages (no block, block with no button, old footer, unreadable, no <article>, no content, healthy), asserts boot 1 rebuilds exactly the broken ones, then asserts boots 2–5 rebuild nothing — across 30 offer-type × destination combinations, including the kill switch.

What a rebuild actually changes (and what it cannot)

restoreFromDB() re-renders the page from blog_posts.content and writes the result to html_content only — that is the single column it updates:


await sb.from('blog_posts').update({ html_content: fixed }).eq('slug', post.slug);

So a rebuild changes the wrapper around the article: <head>/<style>, the meta description, the compact CTA above the article, the closing block, and the "Published … | N min read" line. The article body is passed through sanitizeArticleHTML(content) and embedded verbatim — content, title and slug are never in an UPDATE payload from this path. (This rebuild path predates 2026-10-29; it used to fire only when a page failed the readability audit. Today it also fires on the stale footer, which is why 240 pages moved instead of ~105.)

To verify that on production rather than trust it:


npm run audit:page-fidelity                 # every published post, read-only
npm run audit:page-fidelity -- --slug=chief-of-staff-awareness-1

It compares the article inside each stored page against sanitizeArticleHTML(content) and prints how many are identical, which differ, and which are still on the old footer. It issues SELECTs only. test:blog-html pins the comparison itself (including a tampered-page case), so the audit cannot pass by being blind.

Two guarantees worth stating explicitly:

  1. No published article's page is ever deleted. The prune keeps a file for every

    2. published row (written is built from all of them, not only the rebuilt ones) and only removes files that no published post owns.

  2. The footer rebuild can be switched off entirely with

    3. BLOG_REBUILD_STALE_PAGES=off. With it off, the restore still repairs pages that fail the readability audit — the pre-2026-10-29 behaviour — and leaves every other stored page byte-for-byte as it is.

Verifying a deploy

Checks what a visitor sees, not what the build log claims: the index responds, advertises no verifier page, still lists the corpus; a sample spread across the list all serve, all carry the current footer, and the closing block names the offer with two buttons; and a verifier URL returns 404.

Tests: npm run test:blog-quality (playbook + gate), npm run test:blog-html (CTA block + template), npm run test:blog-fallback (journey titles).

What is not done yet

  1. The 300 existing pages keep their old prose. The template fix (CTA block,

    2. readiness card, top button) reaches them for free via npm run repair:blog-html, which rebuilds HTML from blog_posts.content without a model call.

  2. The readiness card is generic per offer type, not per offer. A job that

    3. needs a specific document should set it on the offer row.

  3. Clicks through a direct provider link are not measurable. The measurement

    4. loop itself exists (GET /tenant/writers-flow/analytics reports views, clicks and CTR per page, and clicks_note says when a zero means "not counted"), but a page whose CTA points straight at the provider is answered by the provider. Closing that gap means routing every CTA through /ai-jobs/apply/<id> — ctaTarget() in utils/ctaBlock.js — which re-renders page frames across the corpus (content untouched, and BLOG_REBUILD_STALE_PAGES=off still applies).

CTA_COVERAGE_2026-10-29.md

docs/CTA_COVERAGE_2026-10-29.md

The button that answered with JSON

Shipped: 2026-10-29 Surface: routes/aiJobs.js (GET /ai-jobs/apply/:id) Test: npm run test:apply-fallback (22 checks) Audit: npm run audit:cta-coverage (read-only, against a live site)

What the log showed


19:57:20  GET /ai-jobs/apply/39fa5490-… 404 - 380ms

Probing that URL returned {"error":"No destination configured for this offer"}. A real reader had pressed a button on a page they read to the end, and the site answered with raw JSON. The click was not recorded either — the route returned before it reached the tracking — so the leak was invisible in the one place that should have shown it.

What it does now

| Before | After | | --- | --- | | 404 {"error":"No destination configured for this offer"} | a page: "Applications for this one are not open yet", the offer's name, a link back to the article and to /ai-jobs | | the click vanished | counted as apply_no_destination (or apply_bad_destination), and recorded on the offer funnel as apply_click with unavailable: true | | a missing offer dropped silently | counted as apply_missing, then the same 404 JSON for API clients |

A working destination still gets a 302, and the destination is still validated (http/https only, and only if it is trusted) before it is used. noindex on the fallback page, so it cannot compete with the real offer in search.

The reason this matters commercially: an "unavailable" click is the most valuable click in the system. It is a reader who wanted to buy something the business forgot to connect. It now appears in the tenant's numbers instead of looking like nobody was interested.

The number that came out of looking

No button at all ....................... 117 (38%)   cannot convert

Three facts, none of which was visible from the database:

  1. Not one page routes its button through this site. Every tracked click the

    2. portal reports will come from future pages, hubs, or offer pages — never from the 191 that already exist. The CTR figure for those pages is not "0%", it is "not measured", which is why the endpoint returns clicks_note.

  2. 117 pages (38%) have no button at all, so they cannot convert however good

    3. the writing is. 96 of those have no closing block whatsoever — the contentJourney "skills/salary" pages were generated by a template that never learned about the CTA.

  3. The two populations want different fixes. The 191 need a tracked redirect; the

    4. 96 need a closing block. Those are template changes and therefore re-render those pages (content untouched, BLOG_REBUILD_STALE_PAGES=off still applies).

What shipped for it (same day)

The two failing populations were invisible to the migration because they were not stale: isStalePage() only matched the pre-2026-10-29 generic footer text, and a page with no closing block at all passes both the readability audit and that check. So the boot restore had never looked at them.

utils/ctaBlock.js now answers the question the migration should have asked:


needsClosingBlock(html)   // has an <article>, but no closing block or nothing to press

Used by:

  • scripts/blogManager.js → restoreFromDB() rebuilds such a page on boot, from

    • its stored content, exactly like the old-footer migration does. BLOG_REBUILD_STALE_PAGES=off still disables it. The log line says which reason fired: [DB] Rebuilt <slug> (no closing block).

  • scripts/repair_blog_html.js → dry-run now lists them, so you can see the list

    • before anything is rewritten: npm run repair:blog-html.

  • scripts/audit_page_fidelity.mjs → new count,

    • no closing block / nothing to press … N, and still verifies the prose is byte-identical to content for every page.

The convergence rule. A predicate that disagrees with the template rebuilds the same page on every single boot, forever. ctaBlock()'s no-destination branch used to emit a block with nothing to press — precisely what the new predicate calls broken. It now ends with <a class="cta-btn cta-btn--alt" href="/ai-jobs">Browse other opportunities</a>, so every branch of the template satisfies the predicate and the repair reaches a fixed point on the first pass. test:blog-html asserts this against the built page, the no-destination page and a page with no offer at all.

What to expect on the next deploy

Up to 117 pages rebuild once (frames only — content untouched, verified by audit:page-fidelity). To see the list before trusting it:


npm run repair:blog-html        # dry run: lists every page it would rebuild, and why
npm run audit:page-fidelity     # prose identical / differs / no closing block
npm run audit:cta-coverage      # after the deploy: no-button count should be 0

The second boot is the one that matters. A rebuild that does not converge rewrites the same pages on every deploy, and the site still looks fine while it happens. That is now a test rather than a hope:


npm run test:boot-convergence   # 11 checks: boot 1 repairs, boots 2-5 do nothing

It boots a corpus of stored pages — no block, block with no button, old footer, unreadable, no <article>, no content, healthy — through the same pageNeedsRebuild() the restore calls, and asserts the second boot rebuilds zero pages across 30 offer-type × destination combinations. I proved the test is not vacuous by removing the no-destination button from ctaBlock(): four checks failed with the exact pages named, then passed again on restore. On Render, the equivalent check is the boot log: the first deploy prints Rebuilt <slug> (no closing block) lines, the second prints none.

If either audit looks wrong, BLOG_REBUILD_STALE_PAGES=off in Render stops the page rebuild entirely and leaves every stored page as it is.

  1. Give the 96 button-less pages a closing block — a third of the corpus

    2. currently persuades and stops. This is the largest conversion gain available, and it is a template fix plus a targeted re-render.

  2. Route the 191 provider links through /ai-jobs/apply/<id> — makes every

    3. existing click countable. ctaTarget() already prefers the tracker when there is no direct URL; it needs to stop preferring the direct URL when there is one.

  3. Report apply_no_destination per tenant in the portal — a "fix these

    4. offers" list, which is a renewal conversation with a number attached.

None of the three should be done by quietly re-rendering the corpus. Each needs the kill switch respected and a before/after diff the owner can read.

What the measurement changed

btn=2 was the wrong metric. A page can carry two buttons and still send the reader nowhere: 56 pages have both buttons pointing at /ai-jobs because their post has no opportunity_id, so ctaBlock() has no offer to name and no destination to resolve. Those pages now say so honestly instead of 404ing, but they cannot convert — they are one opportunity_id away from being both a working destination and a measurement point (/ai-jobs/apply/<id>).

A bug the census exposed, fixed here. The no-destination branch returned the full block before the variant: 'top' check, so a page with an unlinked offer printed the same <h2> and the same "no link configured yet" sentence above the article and again below it. ctaBlock() now honours the compact variant in that branch, repeatsFallbackNote() lets the boot restore repair the 56 pages written while the bug was live, and both test:blog-html and test:boot-convergence assert it.

Content observation, not fixed. The sampled page renders the article title and a Published … | Reading time: 3 min line twice — the template's own, then a copy that lives inside blog_posts.content. That pollution predates this work and no page rebuild can fix it: it would mean rewriting content, the one column every repair path deliberately leaves alone. It needs a separate, reviewed migration.

Next slice, in the order the numbers argue for

  1. 56 pages with no offer link. Match those posts to their opportunity_id.

    2. Each match turns a dead button into /ai-jobs/apply/<id> — a real destination and a click this server can count. Cheapest revenue-adjacent work available.

  2. 378 provider-direct buttons. The reader converts; the server learns nothing.

    3. Routing them through /ai-jobs/apply/<id> (which already records the click and redirects) makes the blog a measurable channel instead of a guess.

  3. The duplicated title inside content. Visible on live pages, needs a

    4. deliberate content migration, not a page rebuild.

Tenant portal — the client's own name, legible copy, Writer's Flow analytics

Surface: admin/tenant_dashboard.html (served at /tenant-dashboard, auth-gated) Backend: server.js → GET /tenant/writers-flow/analytics Test: npm run test:tenant-branding → scripts/test_tenant_branding_analytics.mjs (71 checks) Shipped: 2026-10-27, three changes requested from a live demo of the four tenant links.

---

1. The header wears the client's business name

Before: every tenant saw Tenant Portal in the topbar; their own name sat in the small grey slot on the right.

After: the topbar reads joska Sportiff Academy, Malingu Ventures, lifeline Inc., Canary Onboarding — whatever that client typed into the discovery form. The tab title follows (Scan to Pay · Malingu Ventures | Alphadome) and so does the sign-in card.

Where the name comes from — nothing new had to be stored. POST /discovery saves business_name; approval writes it to bot_tenants.client_name (server.js:15663); every session response already returned it as tenant_name (tenantSessionPayload), and /tenant/session/me rehydrates it after a reload. The page had it in S.name all along and spent it on a 0.88rem grey span.

Implementation. tenantBrand() → applyBrand() is the only writer of the three surfaces (brand, sign-in title, document.title), called from showApp() and showLogin(). Text is set with textContent, so a business name can never inject markup. The phone number moved from being the second copy of the name to the small slot (+254 706 291 262), and on phones it is hidden entirely — the name matters more, and the number is on Profile and in every WhatsApp message the client received.

Measured regression, fixed in the same commit. A grid track declared 1fr is really minmax(auto, 1fr), and that auto floor is the min-content width of the widest item in the column — the topbar. With a real name in it the floor became 431px at a 360px viewport, so the whole document scrolled sideways:

| 360px viewport | document scrollWidth | name shown | | --- | --- | --- | | before the header change | 345px | n/a (Tenant Portal) | | name added, 1fr track | 431px (sideways scroll) | joska Sport… | | name added, minmax(0, 1fr) | 345px | joska Sportiff Academy |

@media (max-width: 1023px) { .app-shell { grid-template-columns: minmax(0, 1fr); } } removes the floor so the header truncates instead of stretching the page. Below 479px the word "Logout" collapses to its icon (still aria-label="Log out", still a 40px target) to hand ~40px back to the name. A 320px phone ellipsises by ~11px; 360px and up show the full name. Verified in headless Chrome at 320/360/375/430/768/1024.

2. Muted copy is legible — measured, not eyeballed

Complaint: "Collect payments for your products and offers…" (Scan to Pay) and the Writer's Flow voice explainer were "almost invisible".

Cause: both are <p class="text-muted">, and Bootstrap 5.3 paints that class #6c757d with !important, so the page palette never reached them. On --bg-card (#181c2f) that is 3.4:1 — below WCAG AA for body text, and hopeless on a phone in daylight.

Fix, in the page's own stylesheet (which loads after the Bootstrap <link>):


.text-muted, .text-body-secondary, .text-secondary, .form-text { color: var(--text-muted) !important; }

A later rule with the same !important wins, and the palette is back in charge. The muted tone itself moved #7c8db0 → #8b9ac0, because 4.98:1 passes on paper but still reads as faint:

| surface | before (#7c8db0) | after (#8b9ac0) | | --- | --- | --- | | --bg-main #0f1117 | 5.66:1 | 6.72:1 | | --bg-card #181c2f | 5.05:1 | 6.00:1 | | --bg-sidebar #13162a | 5.35:1 | 6.36:1 | | Bootstrap's #6c757d on --bg-card — the reported state | 3.59:1 | n/a |

Body copy (--text-light) stays at 12.1:1, so hierarchy survives. The test recomputes all three ratios from the palette with the WCAG formula, so a future "nicer" grey cannot quietly fail AA.

3. Writer's Flow analytics, per tenant

The gap: the owner's /admin/ai-jobs page reports page views, clicks, CTR, posts this week, recent runs and top pages — for the whole platform. The tenant portal's Analytics section reported WhatsApp conversations only, so a client paying for the traffic engine could not see a single number about the pages it wrote for them.

The endpoint: GET /tenant/writers-flow/analytics?days=30 (tenantSessionAuth).

Everything is derived from rows the tenant already owns, so there is no migration:

| number | source | | --- | --- | | offers | opportunities where tenant_id = site_id | | pages, published, words, avg words | blog_posts for those offer ids (content is counted, never returned) | | views, clicks, CTR | analytics_page_views / analytics_clicks where page_path = '/blog/<their slug>' | | series (14 days), top countries | the same view rows, bucketed by day and country | | recent pages | the tenant's newest blog_posts.created_at |

Isolation is structural, not a filter added afterwards: the page-path list is built from the tenant's own slugs, and a row whose path is not in that map is dropped (if (!page) return;). There is no query in the handler without a tenant filter. The list is asked for in batches of 100 because PostgREST caps IN lists. A deployment without blog_posts or the analytics tables answers with zeros plus a note explaining what is missing, rather than a 500 on the section.

The screen: inside Analytics, under the existing conversation panels — 8 headline cards (pages written, live pages, page views, clicks, CTR, words, offers, new pages this week), a Top performing pages table (views / clicks / CTR, each title linking to the public page), a By offer rollup, a 14-day views chart, and the newest pages. The /admin/ai-jobs shape, scoped to one client. A tenant with no pages yet gets a call to action into Writer's Flow instead of a grid of zeros; a failed load says so rather than showing zeros.

Traffic that was never counted (2026-10-29)

Two gaps made the CTR figure — the one number that tells a client the writing works — impossible to move:

1. Clicks were never written. trackClick() existed in utils/analytics.js and had no caller anywhere in the repository. analytics_clicks was therefore always empty, and every tenant's CTR was 0.0% by construction, however well the pages converted. The fix is one line in /ai-jobs/apply/:id, the only place that sees both the reader and the page that sent them:


trackClick(pagePathFromReferrer(req, '/ai-jobs/apply'), 'apply', req).catch(() => {});

pagePathFromReferrer (utils/analytics.js) accepts a referrer only when it is same-host and one of the two shapes this site publishes (/blog/<slug>, /ai-jobs/t/<slug>[/…]), so a stray or spoofed referrer cannot invent traffic on a page the tenant owns. Anything unattributable is recorded as /ai-jobs/apply — it counts for the platform, and is credited to nobody.

2. Hub traffic was invisible. /ai-jobs/t/<slug> recorded views only in opportunity_events, which this portal does not read, so the one page a tenant actually promotes showed zeros. All three tenant routes now also write the visit to analytics_page_views under its real path, and the endpoint reports it as a separate hub object (views, clicks, ctr) matched on the path prefix so the hub's offer and category pages count too.

Hub figures are reported beside the pages, never folded into them: one is the page the tenant hands out, the others are what search finds, and adding them together would hide which is working.

3. A zero now explains itself. When views exist and clicks are zero, the endpoint returns clicks_note and the section prints it:

Page views are counted on every page. Clicks are only counted when the button
goes through this site — a page whose offer links straight to the provider
shows 0 clicks here even if readers clicked it.

That sentence is the difference between "the blog does not convert" and "we could not see this click", and only one of those is true.

Coverage limit, stated plainly: pages whose CTA points at the provider (destination_url / referral_url) are not measurable by this server. Making them measurable means routing every CTA through /ai-jobs/apply/<id>, which changes ctaTarget() in utils/ctaBlock.js and therefore re-renders the page frames of the corpus (content untouched). That is a deliberate, separate decision — not something to slip into a measurement fix.
